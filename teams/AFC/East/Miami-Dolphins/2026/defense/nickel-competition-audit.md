# Finding: Miami has a nickel plan, not yet a nickel winner

## Scope

- Team: Miami Dolphins
- Primary candidate: Chris Johnson
- Other corner candidates: Ethan Robinson and the broader corner rotation
- Package alternatives: a safety as a big nickel; a third corner on passing downs
- Removed from the leading inside projection: Jason Marshall Jr.
- Area: early-down run fits, slot coverage, pressure, communication, package tells, and lineup cost
- Status: active preseason audit
- Last verified: 2026-08-05

## Finding

Miami should not treat the nickel competition as a simple Johnson-versus-Marshall depth-chart decision. The strongest official evidence establishes three different points:

1. Chris Johnson is being trained inside and outside and has the size, tackling temperament, movement, and processing traits Miami wants to test at nickel.
2. Defensive coordinator Sean Duggan views Jason Marshall Jr. primarily as an outside corner after Marshall was moved inside out of need as a rookie.
3. Jeff Hafley has explicitly allowed for different early-down, big-nickel, and third-down occupants if no single defender can handle the entire job.

Johnson is therefore the best-supported candidate to become an every-down or passing-down nickel, but his move inside has an opportunity cost: Miami must then field two other viable outside corners. That is not a minor condition in a room whose opposite-boundary competition is already unsettled.

The current forecast is a package competition:

- **Johnson** has the clearest route to the high-value passing-down assignment and the only publicly confirmed two-position development plan.
- **A larger safety** can enter the early-down or big-nickel mix if Miami values box fits and disguise over pure slot-man coverage.
- **A reserve corner such as Ethan Robinson** has a roster path to third-corner work, but no public first-unit evidence currently supports projecting him as the winner.
- **Marshall** should be evaluated as an outside corner unless actual 2026 deployment overturns Duggan's stated plan.

No source reviewed through August 5 identifies a stable first-unit nickel, an every-down winner, or a repeatable package split.

## Evidence

### Coaching definition of the job

Hafley divided nickel play by situation:

| Situation | Required function | Personnel implication |
|---|---|---|
| Early balanced downs | fit the run, play an open gap or edge, survive motion into the box, blitz, and cover | size, durability, physicality, and communication matter as much as pure corner movement |
| Expected pass | cover a slot receiver in man or zone, including an opponent's best target | a true corner skill set becomes more important |
| Big nickel | add size and safety-like run support without surrendering coverage disguise | a safety can compete for the location even if he is not called the starting nickel |
| Third-corner package | replace some run responsibility with man-coverage ability | a reserve corner can own a narrow but important role |

Hafley said he would prefer one every-down nickel who can satisfy all of those requirements. He also described the fallback: different players and package names for the same apparent location.

That distinction is central. Raw slot snaps alone will not establish one role. The audit must identify down, distance, offensive personnel, coverage, pressure responsibility, and which two corners remain outside.

### Chris Johnson

Johnson is the only candidate whose inside/outside plan has been repeatedly confirmed by Miami:

- Duggan said Johnson is talented enough to play both locations but warned that overloading him could reduce his outside technique.
- Hafley said Johnson was capable of handling multiple positions and would work both.
- Hafley specifically cited Johnson's size and physicality while defining early-down nickel requirements.
- Miami's draft summary described perimeter-to-inside flexibility as a way to vary disguise and personnel.

The dedicated [`chris-johnson-role-audit.md`](chris-johnson-role-audit.md) documents the favorable underlying case: zone vision, movement, 152 college tackles, five forced fumbles, six interceptions, and 23 consecutive starts to finish college.

Those traits make Johnson the most plausible complete answer. They do not prove NFL slot coverage against option routes, traffic, motion, or professional blockers. They also do not solve the boundary lineup when he moves inside.

### Jason Marshall Jr.

Marshall should not remain the default nickel projection merely because the 2025 staff used him there.

Duggan said in May that he sees Marshall more as an outside corner, whether field or boundary. Hafley later noted that Marshall had played more outside in college and that asking a rookie to move inside and outside was difficult.

That view fits Marshall's background:

- 45 Florida games and 39 starts, primarily outside;
- 98 tackles, 27 passes defensed, two interceptions, and one forced fumble;
- 68 completions allowed on 143 college targets, according to Miami's draft profile;
- size and press experience more naturally connected to an outside assignment; and
- a 2025 season interrupted by injured reserve before late-season outside production.

Marshall's eight-tackle, one-interception performance against Tampa Bay showed useful playmaking and tackling in his rookie finale. It did not establish a full-season nickel baseline. A return inside in 2026 would be a real plan change, not the continuation assumption.

Existing hypothesis `mia-2026-def-q13` projects Marshall to lead the nickel competition if Johnson remains outside. Duggan's statement materially weakens that preseason baseline. The hypothesis should remain observable until game deployment, but it should not drive the depth-chart forecast.

### Ethan Robinson and other corner alternatives

Robinson remains on Miami's roster after entering the league as a 2025 undrafted free agent from Minnesota. His 6-foot, 195-pound listing is compatible with slot work, but public official material reviewed for this audit does not establish:

- a 2025 regular-season defensive sample;
- a first-unit 2026 nickel role;
- a recurring inside alignment;
- slot-man or run-fit performance; or
- priority over the other reserve corners.

The same evidence limit applies to moving an outside candidate such as Alex Austin, Ethan Bonner, or another reserve inside. Until Miami repeatedly deploys one there, roster labels and generic versatility are not enough.

### Big-nickel alternatives

Hafley explicitly named big nickel as a possible solution, but he did not identify the player. Miami's unsettled safety group includes defenders with different size, coverage, and communication profiles. A safety entering the slot or box could:

- improve early-down run fits;
- preserve pressure and disguise options;
- reduce the need to teach a reserve outside corner a second location; and
- protect Johnson's outside development.

It could also create a man-coverage mismatch against quick slot receivers. The audit should not assign a safety to this role until deployment is observed.

## Lineup dependencies

### Johnson inside

Miami needs two non-Johnson outside corners on the field simultaneously. This maximizes slot talent but may expose the weaker boundary pair. The defense must compare the slot benefit against the target and safety-help cost outside.

### Johnson outside

Miami preserves its strongest projected boundary corner but must identify another slot defender. A third corner may improve coverage while a safety may improve run support. Either choice can create an identifiable personnel tendency.

### One every-down nickel

A single winner improves continuity, communication, and disguise because personnel does not reveal the likely call. Johnson is the most plausible current candidate, but an every-down inside role would largely abandon his projected outside value.

### Split role

An early-down big nickel plus a passing-down corner matches Hafley's stated fallback. It also risks advertising run/pass intentions and forces communication across more combinations.

## Opponent attack plan to anticipate

- motion the slot into the box and force the nickel to fit like a linebacker;
- use condensed formations, stacks, and bunches to stress releases and handoffs;
- isolate the nickel on option routes with two-way leverage;
- use slot fades, seams, and deep overs against a smaller or slower answer;
- run at a light third-corner package;
- throw against a big-nickel safety in space;
- use tempo to trap the wrong nickel package;
- target the replacement outside corners when Johnson moves inside; and
- identify substitutions that reveal pressure or coverage intent.

## Miami-facing test plan

### Camp and preseason

- Record the nickel on every first-unit snap rather than relying on the listed depth chart.
- Separate early balanced downs, expected pass, third down, two-minute, and red-zone work.
- Record which two defenders remain outside whenever Johnson moves inside.
- Tag corner, safety, and linebacker personnel around the nickel.
- Chart run fits, edge-setting, block defeat, missed tackles, and screen support.
- Chart slot targets by route, depth, leverage, separation, and result.
- Track motion, bunch, switch, pressure-replacement, and late-rotation errors.
- Identify whether nickel substitutions predict pressure, man coverage, or run defense.
- Treat Marshall's first-unit outside work as confirmation of Duggan's plan, not evidence that the nickel is settled.

### Regular season

- Measure Johnson's outside and slot snaps by situation.
- Compare target efficiency and explosive rate at nickel with the boundary cost created by moving Johnson.
- Track early-down success against big nickel and third-corner groupings.
- Compare man-coverage performance against true slot receivers, tight ends, and motioned boundary receivers.
- Measure blitz frequency and pressure production by nickel occupant.
- Reassess after opponents use tempo or repeated formation changes to prevent substitution.

## Fantasy implication

- Opposing slot receivers should not receive a blanket downgrade until Miami identifies and validates its passing-down answer.
- If Johnson becomes the passing-down nickel, opposing slot efficiency could fall while boundary target volume rises against the two corners left outside.
- If Miami uses a safety on early downs, slot receivers may see favorable separation opportunities while rushing matchups become less attractive.
- A predictable split can create opponent play-calling edges and volatile fantasy outcomes even if each defender is functional in isolation.
- Johnson's cornerback-required IDP value rises with slot tackles, blitzes, and short targets, but the added learning load and stronger coverage can suppress predictability.
- No other nickel candidate has a draftable IDP baseline.
- Miami D/ST should receive credit only if the nickel structure preserves disguise without transferring excessive stress to the boundary.

## Registered hypotheses

Five audit-specific hypotheses are registered in [`hypotheses.csv`](hypotheses.csv):

1. Johnson leads passing-down nickel snaps.
2. Miami uses more than one nickel grouping during the first month.
3. Johnson's inside move creates a measurable boundary target cost.
4. A safety receives recurring early-down big-nickel work.
5. Nickel substitutions do not become a reliable offensive tendency tell.

Existing hypotheses `mia-2026-def-q31`, `q34`, and `q35` continue to test Johnson's deployment, learning load, and IDP effect. Existing `q13` is now explicitly treated as a weakened baseline because the current coordinator views Marshall primarily outside.

## Sourcing limitations

- Miami has not published a defensive depth chart with a dedicated nickel winner.
- Public practice reporting reviewed here does not provide complete first-unit slot snap counts.
- College and NFL counting statistics do not isolate slot coverage quality.
- Hafley's package discussion describes possibilities, not the installed frequency.
- No reviewed source names Miami's big-nickel safety.
- Robinson's roster presence establishes candidacy, not role priority or performance.
