# Finding: Miami has complementary pass-rush options, not a proven second engine

## Scope

- Team: Miami Dolphins
- Players: Chop Robinson, Josh Uche, Clelin Ferrell, David Ojabo, Trey Moore, Max Llewellyn, Robert Beal Jr., Cameron Goode, and the interior rush
- Area: individual rush evidence, role fit, four-man combinations, rush-lane integrity, and opponent countermeasures
- Status: preseason audit
- Last verified: 2026-08-05

## Finding

Miami can build a functional pass-rush rotation around Chop Robinson, Zach Sieler, and several specialists. It has not established a second edge defender who can win across situations or independently command protection.

The strongest complementary case belongs to Josh Uche. He has an 11.5-sack, 56-pressure NFL season and a developed speed-and-counter rush plan. That breakout came on 37.8% of New England's defensive snaps, with Matthew Judon helping create one-on-one opportunities, and Uche has not repeated its sack production. He is evidence for a designated pass-rush role, not yet for an every-down solution.

The remaining candidates solve narrower problems:

- Clelin Ferrell can stabilize early downs but has not demonstrated featured NFL sack production.
- David Ojabo retains athletic upside, but injuries and 32 games over four Baltimore seasons make availability and repetition part of the projection.
- Trey Moore has the best multi-year college sack résumé of Miami's rookies, but his production declined after transferring from UTSA to Texas.
- Max Llewellyn offers length, effort, and a productive spin move after a one-year college starting sample.
- Robert Beal Jr., Cameron Goode, and Seth Coleman remain depth or package candidates without evidence sufficient to project a recurring NFL rush role.

The most plausible solution is cumulative: Robinson wins as the lead edge, Uche concentrates on passing downs, Sieler supplies interior pressure, Ferrell protects the run structure, and one developmental rusher earns a small repeatable package. That can produce pressure with four. It also creates substitution tells, injury sensitivity, and no obvious replacement if an offense neutralizes Robinson.

## Candidate audit

### Josh Uche: best pass-down evidence, narrowest proven usage

New England credited Uche with 11.5 sacks and 56 pressures in 2022. Patriots analysis also noted that Judon drew attention and helped Uche receive one-on-one matchups. Uche played only 37.8% of the defensive snaps that season.

In 2023, his sacks fell to two in 12 games. The Patriots reported a 12.2% pass-rush win rate, close to his 12.8% rate in 2022, and cited injury, fewer favorable game scripts, the absence of Judon, and additional protection attention. That distinction matters: the sack fall did not erase every underlying win, but it exposed the conditions around the peak.

Miami reports 21.5 career sacks in 76 games with only four starts through 2025. The demonstrated role is therefore a fresh, specialized rusher whose movement skills can attack a known passing situation. His open tests are:

- whether he stays disciplined against mobile quarterbacks;
- whether he can rush effectively when the protection identifies him;
- whether Miami can use him without conceding the run or telegraphing coverage;
- whether his efficiency survives a larger role; and
- whether he can keep Robinson from receiving routine slides and chips.

### Clelin Ferrell: early-down bridge, not established pressure answer

Washington credited Ferrell with 26 tackles, 3.5 sacks, and one forced fumble in 2024. That is credible rotational production, but it does not make him the passing-down complement.

His value to Miami may be indirect. If Ferrell can set the edge, play the run, and offer enough rush threat to remain on the field occasionally on second-and-long, Miami can avoid an automatic Uche-for-Ferrell substitution. If opponents can infer run or pass from that exchange, the role split becomes an offensive diagnostic tool.

Miami should test Ferrell on:

- first-down play action;
- second-and-medium, where run and pass remain live;
- reduced fronts with Sieler aligned nearby;
- rush games that require timing rather than pure speed; and
- contain responsibility against bootlegs and mobile quarterbacks.

### David Ojabo: an upside bet whose missing evidence is volume

Ojabo produced 11 sacks and five forced fumbles in his final Michigan season, then tore his Achilles at his 2022 pro day. Baltimore placed him on injured reserve with ankle and knee injuries in 2023. He played only five games across his first two NFL seasons.

Miami's official summary credits him with 27 games over 2024–25, which is real improvement in availability, but only 32 games, 4.5 sacks, 10 quarterback hits, and 28 tackles across four total NFL seasons. His 292 defensive snaps and 12 pressures in 2024 were career highs.

The correct prior is neither "bust" nor "breakout." It is a talented, still-developing rusher whose NFL sample has not supported a stable workload. Preseason should answer whether he:

- earns rush opportunities ahead of the rookies and incumbent reserves;
- has a dependable counter after his first move is stopped;
- converts speed to power against NFL tackles;
- maintains edge and rush-lane discipline; and
- can stack practices and games without workload interruption.

### Trey Moore: college production with a role-transition question

Moore recorded 8.0 sacks at UTSA in 2022 and 14.0 in 2023. At Texas, he recorded 5.5 sacks across 16 starts in 2024, then 3.0 in 12 games with five starts in 2025.

The UTSA peak proves he can finish at the college level. The decline against a different schedule, within a deeper roster, and with fewer 2025 starts creates an open translation question rather than a clean upward trend.

Miami lists him as EDGE/ILB. That flexibility could unlock pressure disguise, but every added assignment can compete with development of an NFL-caliber rush plan. His first useful role may be selected pressure packages, edge-setting relief, and special teams rather than immediate second-rusher volume.

### Max Llewellyn: a specific tool and a small résumé

Llewellyn did not start until his fifth college season. Miami reported 45 pressures, a 14.7% pressure rate, and 6.5 sacks in 2025. It also credited his spin move with 27 pressures over his final two college seasons.

That gives Miami a real development trait: a long edge defender with a productive counter and persistent motor. It does not prove he can win the corner, anchor against NFL run blocks, or deploy the spin after professional tackles anticipate it. His path begins with roster value and a limited package.

## Interior contribution

The complementary audit cannot stop at the opposite edge. Sieler is Miami's most proven pass-rush finisher: consecutive 10-sack seasons in 2023–24 followed by 5.5 in 2025. He can align across the front and create the shortest route to the quarterback.

Kenneth Grant's two rookie sacks are a starting point, not proof of a passing-down pairing. Miami needs to test whether Grant can:

- push the pocket without opening escape lanes;
- occupy a center-guard combination and leave Sieler isolated;
- execute tackle-end games with Robinson or Uche;
- stay on the field when the offense spreads; and
- finish after initial disruption.

If Grant develops, Miami can force protections to account for both interior gaps and make Robinson's edge matchup cleaner. If he remains mostly a run defender, Miami may need a lighter interior package whose pass-rush gain carries a run-defense cost.

## Four-man combinations to test

| Situation | Plausible four | Purpose | Failure signal |
|---|---|---|---|
| Balanced early down | Robinson–Sieler–Grant–Ferrell | preserve run integrity without surrendering all rush threat | play action consistently creates clean pockets |
| Second-and-long | Robinson–Sieler–Grant–Uche | add speed while keeping interior mass | offenses run successfully at Uche or isolate Grant in space |
| Obvious pass | Robinson–Sieler–Uche–Ojabo/Moore | maximize four individual rush threats | pocket opens inside or quarterback escapes through undisciplined lanes |
| Simulated pressure | three front players plus a linebacker/DB, with an edge dropping | alter the protection while rushing four | coverage loses the dropping edge or the rush creates no one-on-one |
| Mobile-quarterback plan | Robinson–Sieler–Grant–Ferrell/Moore | compress with controlled lanes | speed rushers climb past the quarterback and open scramble lanes |

These are test combinations, not reported Miami packages.

## Opponent countermeasures Miami must solve

1. **Slide or chip toward Robinson.** The opposite edge must win a one-on-one or the protection has removed Miami's lead threat cheaply.
2. **Run at Uche or a light package.** Passing-down personnel must survive draws, scrambles, and tendency breakers.
3. **Quick game.** Pressure rate has limited value if the quarterback can release before the rush arrives; coverage leverage and batted-pass opportunities matter.
4. **Max protection.** Miami must cover a reduced route menu without sending unnecessary extra rushers.
5. **Screen and boot action.** Pursuit discipline must survive attempts to exploit rush aggression.
6. **Quarterback movement.** Four-man pressure must maintain lane spacing rather than accumulate harmless edge wins.
7. **Tempo.** Miami needs a personnel group that can defend multiple situations when substitution is unavailable.

## What is proven, inferred, and open

### Verified facts

- Robinson owns the strongest recent Miami edge production among current players.
- Uche has produced one double-digit-sack NFL season in a part-time role.
- Sieler has multiple double-digit-sack seasons from the interior.
- Ojabo's first four NFL seasons were low-volume and affected by major injuries.
- Moore and Llewellyn enter the NFL with productive but distinct college samples.

### Analytical inferences

- Uche is the best current candidate to complement Robinson on passing downs.
- Ferrell's largest value may be preventing the early-down structure from becoming predictable.
- A committee is more plausible than one new full-time opposite edge.
- Interior development from Grant would improve every edge projection.

### Open questions

- Who wins after opponents shift protection to Robinson?
- Can Uche preserve rush efficiency above a specialist workload?
- Can any developmental edge earn a recurring third-down role?
- Can the four-man rush keep disciplined lanes against mobile quarterbacks?
- Can Miami change personnel without announcing run or pass?
- Does the rush create pressure quickly enough to protect the secondary?
- Does it still work after opponents use chips, quick game, screens, and max protection?

## Miami-facing test plan

### Camp and preseason

- Record first-unit edge pair by down, distance, and offensive personnel.
- Chart every Uche–Ferrell exchange and the next offensive play type.
- Separate individual wins from free rushers created by scheme.
- Record where the protection slides and which rusher receives chips.
- Track pressure within 2.5 seconds separately from late cleanup pressure.
- Grade rush-lane integrity on every scramble and designed boot.
- Identify interior passing-down personnel and all tackle-end games.
- Track Ojabo, Moore, and Llewellyn opportunity, win, counter, and contain rates rather than sacks alone.

### Regular season

- Recheck four-man pressure after 50 and 100 opponent dropbacks.
- Split performance before and after the opponent's first major protection adjustment.
- Compare pressure with and without Uche and with and without Grant.
- Track quick-game rate, chip rate, max-protection rate, and Robinson double-team attention.
- Separate sack conversion from repeatable disruption.
- Reassess fantasy conclusions after mobile-quarterback and quick-game opponents.

## Fantasy implication

- Robinson retains the best individual sack ceiling, but a weak complement would increase protection attention and weekly volatility.
- Uche can matter to Miami D/ST without playing enough snaps for stable IDP value.
- Ferrell can improve the defense while remaining a low-volume fantasy player.
- Ojabo and the rookies are watch-list players until a recurring package and snap floor appear.
- Sieler remains the safest defensive-line IDP because his role and multi-year production are established.
- Miami D/ST needs pressure that survives four-man deployment and opponent adjustment. Scheme-created free rushers alone are not a durable projection.
- Failed lane discipline raises quarterback rushing and scramble-drill receiving value even if Miami records pressures.

## Sourcing limitations

- Public pressure, win-rate, and snap figures come from different charting systems and are not directly interchangeable.
- Team-authored analysis supplies useful factual access but may emphasize favorable traits.
- Sack totals do not measure time to pressure, cleanup production, protection attention, or lane discipline.
- No representative 2026 game sample exists. Camp one-on-ones favor rushers and do not reproduce game protections.
- Ferrell's post-2024 production and exact 2026 role require continued verification as Miami publishes preseason material.
- Public evidence is insufficient to project Beal, Goode, Coleman, or other reserves beyond competition status.

## Assessment

- Confidence: medium in the candidate hierarchy; low in final snap and package distribution.
- Reason: the veteran histories and rookie production are documented, but Miami has not supplied representative deployment.
- Fact/inference boundary: cited production, transactions, injuries, and dated observations are evidence. Role hierarchy, combinations, and opponent responses are hypotheses.
- What would invalidate this: an edge other than Uche wins a broad complementary role; Ferrell becomes a primary passing-down rusher; Grant or another interior defender becomes the clear second pressure engine; or Miami relies routinely on five-plus rushers.
- Next review: after each preseason game; after 50 and 100 regular-season opponent dropbacks.
